# Overview & Purpose

This skill guides the translation of an abstract chapter outline into a relentless, beat-by-beat narrative draft. Great novels are not written in sprawling, unfocused paragraphs; they are assembled from tight, kinetic scenes where characters are forced into physical and psychological action.

The agent uses this skill to:
- Structure scenes using the **Butcher & Swain Scene-and-Sequel Engine** (Proactive Scene: Goal → Conflict → Disaster; Reactive Sequel: Reaction → Dilemma → Decision).
- Apply the **"In Late, Out Early"** rule to eliminate dead time, transitional throat-clearing, and polite chit-chat.
- Inject **Micro-Tension** on every page so that even quiet, conversational, or expository moments carry friction.
- Escalate conflicts through **"Yes, but..." and "No, and furthermore..."** outcome variations instead of binary successes.

## When to Use & Trigger Phrases

**Activate this skill when:**
- Drafting a specific scene from a chapter outline.
- A draft scene feels boring, sluggish, or static.
- Breaking down an action sequence, interrogation, heist, or romantic confrontation into cinematic beats.
- Eliminating unnecessary character travel, polite greetings, or repetitive exits.
- Calibrating the emotional rhythm between high-octane action and contemplative character recovery.

**Example trigger phrases:**
- "Help me beat out this confrontation scene"
- "Draft Scene 1 of Chapter 4 with high tension"
- "This conversation feels flat; give it better micro-beats"
- "How do I structure the beats for a swordfight/interrogation/heist?"
- "Cut the fluff from this scene using 'In Late, Out Early'"
- "Apply Swain's Scene and Sequel to my draft"

## Core Literary Methodology & Rules

### 1. The Proactive Scene Engine (Action & Collision)
Every action-driven scene follows a tripartite causal loop:
1. **Concrete Goal**: The POV character must enter the scene wanting something immediate, visual, and measurable within the room (e.g., steal the ledger, force a confession, escape through the window). *A vague desire like "figure out what's going on" is prohibited.*
2. **Dynamic Conflict**: The obstacles must actively push back. The obstacle can be human (an opponent), environmental (rising floodwaters), or institutional (a countdown timer). The character makes multiple attempts, each met with resistance.
3. **The Disaster (The Climax of the Beat)**: The scene ends NOT with clean victory, but with one of two complications:
   - **"No, and furthermore..."**: They fail to achieve the goal, AND a new, worse problem is inflicted upon them.
   - **"Yes, but..."**: They achieve the immediate goal, BUT doing so costs them something vital or triggers a catastrophic unintended consequence.

### 2. The Reactive Sequel Engine (Processing & Reset)
Following a disaster, characters cannot immediately sprint into the next battle without emotional exhaustion and strategic recalibration:
1. **Reaction**: The visceral, instinctive, physical and emotional response to the disaster (shock, nausea, tears, adrenaline crash).
2. **Dilemma**: The realization that the old plan is dead and no easy options remain. The character weighs terrible options (Option A will ruin their reputation; Option B will risk their ally’s life).
3. **Decision**: The character commits to a new, active choice. This decision becomes the **Goal** for the subsequent Proactive Scene.

### 3. "In Late, Out Early" (The Razor Rule)
- **In Late**: Never start a scene when the character wakes up, drives across town, parks the car, greets the receptionist, or knocks on the door. Start the scene *at the moment the conflict has already begun or is 5 seconds from detonating* (e.g., as the door flies off its hinges; mid-argument).
- **Out Early**: Cut the scene the instant the climactic disaster or critical decision occurs. Do not linger while characters say goodbye, wash their hands, or tidy up the room.

### 4. Micro-Tension on Every Page (Donald Maass Principle)
Even during quiet scenes, tension must hum beneath the surface:
- Conflict between internal thoughts and external spoken words.
- Physical discomfort in the setting (stifling heat, an unscratchable itch under armor, a ticking grandfather clock).
- Interpersonal friction over petty details that mirror larger thematic differences.

## Step-by-Step Execution Protocol

1. **Step 1: Define the Scene Anchor Matrix**
   - **POV**: Who holds the lens?
   - **Goal**: What does the character want to leave this room holding or knowing?
   - **Antagonist in the Scene**: Who or what is preventing them from getting it?
   - **Stakes**: What is the immediate penalty for failure?

2. **Step 2: Find the True Starting Point ("In Late")**
   - Strike the first 2-3 paragraphs of setup. Find the exact sentence where friction ignites and open there.

3. **Step 3: Map the 4–6 Micro-Beats**
   - *Beat 1 (The Approach & First Gambit)*: Character states or acts toward goal; obstacle parries.
   - *Beat 2 (Escalation / Tactic Shift)*: Character changes strategy (from charm to bribery, or stealth to force); stakes raise.
   - *Beat 3 (The Tipping Point)*: A secret slips, a weapon is drawn, or a deadline expires.
   - *Beat 4 (The Disaster / "Yes, but..." or "No, and furthermore...")*: The irreversible complication hits.

4. **Step 4: Execute the "Out Early" Cut**
   - Terminate the scene on the shockwave of the disaster or the sharp edge of the decision. Leave the reader hanging over the precipice.

## Quality Checklist & Verification Criteria

- [ ] Does the scene enter late, bypassing pleasantries, routine travel, and morning routines?
- [ ] Does the POV character have a clear, active micro-objective within the first two paragraphs?
- [ ] Does the scene resolve with a "Yes, but..." or a "No, and furthermore..." outcome rather than a frictionless victory?
- [ ] Is there micro-tension present during every block of dialogue and description?
- [ ] Are physical actions and somatic responses grounded in the character's physical state (injuries, fatigue, adrenaline)?
- [ ] Does the scene cut immediately after the punchline or disaster without lingering throat-clearing?

## Anti-Patterns & Prohibited AI Tropes

- **The "Chit-Chat Warmup"**: Opening scenes with polite greetings ("Good morning, Lord Vance," "Good morning to you, Captain. Lovely weather today, isn't it?").
- **The Frictionless Success**: The protagonist attempts a difficult action, succeeds on the first try, and walks away smiling with no complication.
- **The Head-Bobbing Echo**: Characters endlessly nodding, grinning, chuckling, or murmuring "indeed" in agreement rather than contesting conversational dominance.
- **The Floating White Room**: Scenes where characters speak pages of dialogue without interacting with physical props, furniture, terrain, or somatic sensations.
- **Over-stayed Welcome**: Continuing the scene for three paragraphs after the climax to show characters tidying up, walking to their cars, or having a debrief that repeats what just happened.

## Concrete Examples (Before vs. After Craft Application)

### Example 1: Interrogation / Negotiation Scene

**Before (Polite, Slow, and Frictionless):**
> Marcus walked down the stone corridor of the dungeon. He unlocked the heavy iron door with his key ring and stepped inside. Inquisitor Drake was sitting at a wooden table looking over some documents.  
> "Hello, Drake," Marcus said.  
> "Good evening, Marcus," Drake replied, setting down his quill. "What brings you to my office at this late hour?"  
> "I need the manifest for the southern armory shipment," Marcus explained.  
> "That information is classified by order of the Grand Marshal," Drake told him with a frown.  
> "Please, Drake. It's really important for my investigation. I have reason to believe someone is selling weapons to the rebels."  
> Drake sighed and opened his drawer. "Well, since you put it that way, here is the ledger. Be careful with it."  
> "Thank you, Drake. I will return it tomorrow," Marcus said, taking the ledger and walking back down the hallway to his study.

**After (In Late, High Stakes, Tactical Friction, and "Yes, But..." Disaster):**
> The wax seal on Drake’s desk was still smoking when Marcus slammed both palms onto the parchment, pinning the inquisitor's sleeve to the mahogany.  
> "You sign that warrant," Marcus whispered, "and three hundred conscripts die in the ironworks before dawn."  
> Drake didn't flinch. He let the molten green wax drip onto the cuff of his linen shirt, his gray eyes fixed on Marcus's unbuttoned coat where the stolen guard captain's cipher rested.  
> "The Grand Marshal has already signed the requisition, Captain," Drake said smoothly. "If you want the southern manifest, you’ll have to cut my fingers off the desk to get it."  
> Marcus twisted his wrist, pressing the cold rim of his flintlock against the underside of Drake's jaw until the bone clicked. "Don't tempt a man with seven hours left to live."  
> Drake’s pulse fluttered against the steel barrel, but a thin, bloodless smile touched his lips. He slid the black leather ledger across the blotter with his free hand.  
> "Take it," Drake rasped. "Every name and serial number is on page forty. But you might want to look at the seal on the back cover first."  
> Marcus snatched the book, his thumb catching the broken lead clasp. Emblazoned in gold leaf across the binding was the crest of the Red Hand Cartel—bearing the personal wax signature of Marcus's own sister.  
> Bootsteps thudded in the stairwell outside. Iron keys rattled against the lock.  
> Drake smiled wider. "They’re ten minutes early. I suggest you decide whether you're jumping out the second-story window or hanging alongside your family."

### Example 2: Scene with Swain Sequel (Reaction to Disaster)

**Before (Shallow Emotional Recovery):**
> After the bomb exploded, Elena felt very shocked and sad. She looked at the smoke and wondered why this had happened to her. She decided she couldn't give up because the city depended on her. She picked up her radio to call her boss and tell him she was going to hunt down the bomber.

**After (Visceral Somatic Reaction, Brutal Dilemma, Hard Decision):**
> Elena fell to her knees in the wet gravel, the concrete dust coating her tongue like bitter chalk. Her right eardrum was whistling—a shrill, flat pitch that drowned the siren wails from the avenue. She clamped her hand over her ribs, gasping as jagged glass ground inside her leather tactical vest.  
> On the asphalt twenty yards away lay the burned steering column of the evacuation van. Her squad wasn't calling out. No one was crawling out of the ditch.  
> Her fingers trembled so violently she dropped the frequency scrambler into the slush twice. Two choices remained, each a death sentence:  
> If she called the tactical precinct for medical backup, the corrupt division commander would trace the transponder and execute every survivor in the cellar safehouse within forty minutes. If she turned her back on the burning van and fled on foot to secure the safehouse herself, her sergeant would bleed out in the snow before the fire trucks arrived.  
> She looked at the blood spreading across her knees. She dragged the frequency scrambler under her boot heel and crushed it into copper splinters.  
> "I'm sorry, Sarge," she breathed into the sulfur wind, and started running toward the tenements.
