# Overview & Purpose

This skill transforms dialogue from a functional delivery vehicle for plot exposition into a psychological battlefield. In real human interaction, people rarely say exactly what they mean. They protect vulnerabilities, weaponize polite etiquette, deploy deflections, pursue hidden agendas, and signal social dominance or submission through pacing, silence, and micro-actions.

The agent uses this skill to:
- Separate **Spoken Text** (the literal words uttered) from **Subtext** (the unspoken emotional truth, fear, or tactical demand).
- Map and shift **Conversational Power Dynamics** across every exchange (who leads, who yields, who interrupts, who controls the silence).
- Build unique **Character Idiolects** (vocabulary constraints, cadence, metaphoric roots, sentence structure) so characters never sound interchangeable.
- Eliminate **"Talking Heads in a Void"** by integrating physical stage business, props, and environmental friction into speech beats.

## When to Use & Trigger Phrases

**Activate this skill when:**
- Characters sound too polite, overly direct, or "on-the-nose" (stating their feelings and plot facts plainly).
- All characters sound like the same intelligent, articulate authorial voice.
- An argument, interrogation, seduction, or negotiation scene lacks dramatic tension.
- Dialogue tags are cluttered with melodramatic adverbs ("he said angrily," "she whispered softly").
- Two characters converse for pages with no physical action, somatic grounding, or environmental interaction.

**Example trigger phrases:**
- "Make this dialogue less on-the-nose"
- "Inject subtext into this argument between [Character A] and [Character B]"
- "Help me differentiate their speaking styles and accents/idiolects"
- "How can they threaten each other without saying 'I'll kill you'?"
- "Clean up these dialogue tags and add action beats"
- "Increase the conversational tension in this dinner scene"

## Core Literary Methodology & Rules

### 1. The Text vs. Subtext Dichotomy
- **Text (The Iceberg Tip)**: What the character vocalizes aloud. Often polite, evasive, mundane, sarcastic, or falsely cooperative.
- **Subtext (The Deep Current)**: The real emotional engine. It is what the character *cannot afford to say out loud* because of social consequence, shame, terror, or tactical disadvantage.
- *Rule*: Never let a character state their deepest wound or desire explicitly unless they are completely shattered at the climax. If a character says *"I feel unloved and insecure,"* it is psychology homework, not fiction. If they inspect their teacup and whisper *"You bought the cheap Darjeeling again,"* it is subtextual cruelty.

### 2. Conversational Tactics & Power Dynamics
Every conversation is an negotiation for dominance, survival, or intimacy. Characters cycle through tactical gambits:
- **The Deflection**: Answering a question with another question, changing the subject, or feigning deafness.
- **The Stonewall (Weaponized Silence)**: Forcing the opponent to fill uncomfortable pauses until they babble and expose their hand.
- **The Flattery Trap**: Showering the adversary with exaggerated compliments to disarm them before slipping in a poisoned barb.
- **The Double Entendre / Coded Threat**: Using legal, domestic, or culinary metaphors to deliver lethal ultimatums.
- **The Interruption / Stealing Breath**: Cutting off another's sentence to assert superior social rank.

### 3. The Character Idiolect Matrix
To prevent the "Universal Author Voice," assign each primary character distinct linguistic parameters:
1. **Sentence Cadence & Length**: Do they speak in terse, clipped monosyllables (working-class soldier) or rolling, subordinate-clause periods (courtly bureaucrat)?
2. **Metaphor Source**: Where do their analogies originate?
   - A blacksmith compares patience to *tempering spring steel*.
   - A surgeon speaks of *excising necrosis, cauterizing, and tourniquets*.
   - A card cheat speaks in *tells, odds, bluffs, and folding*.
3. **Taboo Words & Habits**: What words do they refuse to speak? Do they drop the pronoun ("Went downtown. Saw the mess.") or overuse passive voice to avoid admitting fault?

### 4. Action Beats over Dialogue Tags
Adverbs attached to dialogue tags (`he said angrily`, `she said sarcastically`) are amateur crutches. If the line requires an adverb to explain its tone, the dialogue itself has failed.
- **Strike Dialogue Adverbs**: Replace `said angrily` with a physical action beat that conveys anger through prop interaction.
- *Weak*: `"Don't ever speak to me again," she said furiously.*
- *Strong*: *She set the porcelain teacup into the saucer so hard the rim chipped. "Pour yourself another cup. I'm leaving."*

## Step-by-Step Execution Protocol

1. **Step 1: Define the Two Agendas (Text vs. Subtext)**
   - For Speaker A: State their surface topic (e.g., selling horse grain) and their true subtext (e.g., warning Speaker B that the sheriff knows about the murder).
   - For Speaker B: State their surface topic (e.g., haggling over price) and their true subtext (e.g., testing whether Speaker A has already taken a bribe).

2. **Step 2: Strip On-the-Nose Declarations**
   - Identify any line where a character directly names their emotion ("I am scared," "I hate you," "I am jealous").
   - Transpose the emotion into an indirect object: a dispute over food, an unreturned book, an insult directed at a third party, or excessive politeness.

3. **Step 3: Choreograph Physical Stage Business (Props & Terrain)**
   - Give both characters something to do with their hands (lighting a cigar, paring an apple, cleaning a rifle, folding laundry, shuffling cards).
   - Use micro-actions to puncture or contradict spoken dialogue (e.g., saying *"I'm totally relaxed"* while snapping a matchstick in half).

4. **Step 4: Audit for Idiolect Integrity**
   - Read each character’s lines in isolation. Cover up the character names. Can you immediately identify who is speaking purely from word choice, rhythm, and sentence length?

5. **Step 5: Trim the Dialogue Fat**
   - Strike empty greetings ("Hello," "How are you?"), repetitive confirmations ("Yes," "I agree"), and expository summaries of things both characters already know.

## Quality Checklist & Verification Criteria

- [ ] Does the dialogue avoid on-the-nose declarations of emotion and exposition?
- [ ] Is there a clear, discernible subtext beneath the literal words being exchanged?
- [ ] Do both characters use distinct sentence structures, cadences, and metaphoric roots?
- [ ] Are dialogue tags minimal (`said`, `asked`) and completely stripped of melodramatic adverbs?
- [ ] Are speech lines interwoven with physical props, somatic tension, and spatial movement?
- [ ] Does the conversational balance of power shift at least once during the scene?

## Anti-Patterns & Prohibited AI Tropes

- ❌ **The As-You-Know-Bob Infodump**: Characters recounting their own shared history or plot facts just to educate the reader (*"As you know, Marcus, ever since our father died five years ago in the Great War of the North..."*).
- ❌ **The Dialogue Adverb Explosion**: Relying on adverbs like `vehemently`, `plaintively`, `menacingly`, `wryly`, or `scornfully` after `said`.
- ❌ **The Perfectly Articulate Debater**: Every character having an immediate, witty, Oscar Wilde-level comeback without pausing, stammering, deflecting, or interrupting.
- ❌ **The Melodramatic Soap Opera Confession**: Characters giving grand speeches confessing their childhood trauma while in the middle of a tactical firefight or casual lunch.
- ❌ **The Floating Dialogue Void**: Pages of quotes with zero action beats, leaving the reader unable to visualize where the characters are standing or what their bodies are doing.

## Concrete Examples (Before vs. After Craft Application)

### Example 1: Estranged Father and Daughter in a Diner

**Before (On-the-Nose, Expository, Emotionally Direct):**
> "Hi, Dad," Maya said, sitting down in the booth. "I haven't seen you in three years."  
> "Hello, Maya," Arthur said sadly. "I know I was a terrible father who drank too much and neglected you, but I want to make amends."  
> "I don't know if I can forgive you, Dad," Maya said angrily. "You broke Mom's heart, and you were never there for my graduations or birthdays. I feel very bitter about my childhood."  
> "I understand your anger," Arthur replied remorsefully. "I am clean now, and I just want a second chance to be in your life."

**After (Subtext, Prop Interaction, Power Dynamics, Unspoken Pain):**
> Maya slipped into the vinyl booth, leaving her coat zipped to the chin. She slid her car keys onto the Formica counter, right next to the salt shaker, leaving the engine alarm fob facing her thumb.  
> Arthur didn't reach for her hands. He pushed a paper napkin dispenser half an inch to the left, aligning it with the edge of the laminate table. The skin around his knuckles was scrubbed raw—red, clean, smelling of cheap laundry bleach instead of stale rye.  
> "You're driving the Corolla," he said.  
> "Tire pressure's fine," Maya said. She didn't open the laminated menu. "You ordered coffee."  
> "Decaf."  
> "Since when?"  
> Arthur tore the paper sleeve of a toothpick into four equal ribbons, laying each strip parallel on the napkin. "Doctor said the old pump doesn't like the rush anymore."  
> "The doctor in St. Paul, or the doctor at the county shelter?"  
> A muscle jumped along Arthur’s jaw. He placed the fourth strip of paper down. "They serve the same soup either way, kiddo."  
> Maya picked up her keys. The metal jingled against her silver ring. "I have twenty minutes before the meter expires, Arthur. Say what's in your pocket so I can leave."

### Example 2: Corrupt Official and a Detective (Hidden Threat)

**Before (Direct & Cliché Threat):**
> "Detective Ross, I advise you to stop your investigation into my offshore accounts," Councilman Vance said angrily. "If you don't drop the charges, I will use my political influence to ruin your career and get you fired from the police department!"  
> "You can't threaten me, Vance!" Ross shouted bravely. "I have the evidence, and I will put you behind bars!"

**After (Coded Language, Etiquette as a Weapon, Ice-Cold Subtext):**
> Councilman Vance uncapped his silver fountain pen and signed the zoning authorization without glancing up from the vellum.  
> "Your nephew is at St. Jude's Academy, isn't he, Detective?"  
> Ross kept his hands behind his back, fingers hooked into his belt. "He plays junior varsity soccer."  
> "A lovely school," Vance murmured. He blotted the signature with a green felt rocker. "Quite an expansive sports field. Backs right up against the municipal rail spur. The city has been reviewing maintenance subsidies for the grade crossings along that corridor. Aging signals, budget deficits... accidents can happen so dreadfully fast when the warning bells fail to chime."  
> Vance set the pen down. The polished barrel rolled two inches across the desk and stopped against Ross's case file.  
> "I'd hate to see a promising boy's season cut short by administrative oversight, Detective Ross. Wouldn't you?"  
> Ross looked at the file under the pen. "The grade crossings have been inspected twice this month, Councilman."  
> Vance smiled, his eyes cold as well-water. "Then perhaps a third inspection would be redundant. Enjoy your afternoon, Detective. My secretary will validate your parking."
